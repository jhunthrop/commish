# Model v1, measured against the floor

**Measured 2026-08-27.** Reproduce with `npm run projections:backfill`, `npm run projections:fit`,
then `npm run projections:report 2024` and `npm run projections:report 2023`.
Spec: `docs/design/2026-08-27-projection-engine-spec.md`. Floor: `docs/research/2026-08-27-naive-baseline.md`.

This is the first thing the engine has built that can be right or wrong. Phase 1 measured the floor;
this measures a model against it, on two seasons no parameter has ever seen.

## What the model is

Three layers, each small enough to argue with.

**Layer 1, opportunity** predicts *shares* — snap, target, carry and pass-attempt — through a
two-level shrinkage: a position's role prior, then the player's prior-season mean shrunk toward it,
then this season's recency-weighted form shrunk toward that. One decaying average cannot do this
job: at any decay that makes last week matter, a week eighteen weeks back is worth nothing, so a
week-1 prediction would be pure role prior and would lose to "what he did last year". Two levels
answer honestly at both ends.

The role prior *is* the depth chart. We have no historical depth charts, but snap share is one made
numeric — a 90%-snap receiver is a number one and a 25%-snap receiver is not — so every other share
regresses toward a per-position slope against the predicted snap share.

**Layer 2, efficiency** regresses each per-opportunity rate toward its role's mean, counted in
opportunities rather than games, so a back with two hundred carries and a back with twenty are not
treated as equally known.

**Layer 3, composition** multiplies volume by rate into a predicted `StatLine`, which the league's
own rules turn into points. Nothing in it knows what a point is worth.

## How the held-out discipline was enforced

| seasons | role |
|---|---|
| 2020 | history only — nflverse publishes no `stats_player_week_2019.csv`, so 2020 has no prior season of its own |
| 2021, 2022 | **fit** — every parameter comes from these two and nothing else |
| 2023, 2024 | **held out** — never seen by any fit; every number below is measured on them |

`fitOpportunityPriors` and `fitEfficiencyPriors` take the held-out list explicitly and **throw** if
asked to fit on a season named in it. The fence is code, not intention. The fitted parameters are
generated into a committed `src/projections/model/fitted.ts`, so "what did the engine believe when it
said that" is answerable from the repository alone.

## The headline

2024 regular season, PPR, replayed week by week. **Like for like** — restricted to the players *both*
sources projected, so neither is credited for breadth.

| | rho | MAE | RMSE | n |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| naive floor | 0.569 | 5.12 | 7.00 | 4,766 |
| **model v1** | **0.677** | **4.58** | **6.37** | 4,766 |

And the same on the second held-out season, 2023:

| | rho | MAE | RMSE | n |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| naive floor | 0.561 | 5.06 | 6.77 | 4,677 |
| **model v1** | **0.678** | **4.46** | **6.15** | 4,677 |

**+0.108 rank correlation in 2024 and +0.117 in 2023**, on two independent seasons, with error down
about 11% on both. The engine's rule is that a result needs two independent seasons or it is noise;
this one has them.

### On breadth, and why the headline is the restricted number

Scored over the players each source *chose* to have an opinion about, the model reads better still —
rho 0.687 on 5,685 player-weeks in 2024 against the floor's 0.569 on 4,767. That number is real but
it is not a like-for-like comparison. The naive benchmark is structurally blind to anyone without a
prior season; the model projects anyone with usage history, including rookies. Those extra
player-weeks are overwhelmingly low scorers who are easy to order correctly, and they flatter rank
correlation on breadth rather than on accuracy.

The gap between the two is how much of the win was coverage: **0.687 unrestricted against 0.677
restricted, so about a tenth of the improvement is breadth and nine tenths is accuracy.** Both are
reported by `replayHeadToHead`; the restricted one is the claim.

## By rank band — including what got worse

Like for like, 2024. Bands are by each source's own projected rank, so a band-by-band comparison
between sources is indicative rather than exact (the fix is banding by a common prior, which arrives
with the ADP benchmark). And within-band rho is depressed by range restriction: **compare band to
band, never band to the overall row.**

| band | rank | floor rho | model rho | Δ | floor MAE | model MAE |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| elite | 1–50 | 0.045 | 0.069 | **+0.024** | 7.50 | 6.65 |
| starter | 51–150 | 0.292 | 0.175 | **−0.117** | 6.27 | 6.25 |
| deep | 151–400 | 0.265 | 0.351 | **+0.086** | 4.54 | 4.11 |
| fringe | 401+ | 0.186 | 0.250 | **+0.064** | 2.84 | 1.89 |

**The starter band regressed, and it is the most important line in this document.** Rank correlation
among players ranked 51–150 fell from 0.292 to 0.175 — the model orders that band *worse* than
"last season's points per game" does, while beating it everywhere else. Absolute error is flat there
too (6.25 against 6.27), the only band where the model does not also reduce error.

The 2023 numbers say the regression did **not** reproduce:

| band | 2023 floor rho | 2023 model rho | Δ |
|---|---|---|---|
| elite | 0.154 | 0.165 | +0.011 |
| starter | 0.087 | 0.221 | **+0.134** |
| deep | 0.181 | 0.322 | +0.141 |
| fringe | 0.068 | 0.276 | +0.208 |

So by the engine's own two-season rule, **the starter regression is one season of evidence and is
not yet a finding** — but neither is it dismissible, because 2024 is the season the floor was
originally published on and the one anybody will check. It is named here rather than averaged away,
and it is the first segment Phase 3 should cut error by.

The `deep` band is where the spec expected the first real win — "human analyst attention runs out in
the deep pool, and that is where leagues are won" — and it delivered on both seasons: +0.086 and
+0.141, over 2,059 and 1,884 scored player-weeks. That is the result the phase was designed to find.

## The decay curve — the most informative single result

The naive floor decays through a season because it never stops reading January. Whether the model
flattens that curve is the direct test of whether its in-season features do anything at all.

| | weeks 1–9 | weeks 10–18 | direction |
|---|---|---|---|
| floor, 2024 | 0.587 | 0.555 | decays |
| **model, 2024** | **0.669** | **0.710** | **improves** |
| floor, 2023 | 0.581 | 0.542 | decays |
| **model, 2023** | **0.668** | **0.667** | **flat** |

The curve is not merely flattened, it is **reversed in 2024 and flat in 2023**. The model's advantage
grows as the season goes on: +0.004 behind in week 1, then +0.098 by week 3, +0.148 by week 7,
+0.195 by week 12 and +0.198 by week 17. That is exactly the shape predicted when the floor was
measured — "any model that reads the current season at all should beat the naive floor by more in
November than in September, and if it does not, its in-season features are doing nothing."

Week 1 is the one week the model does not win (0.678 against 0.682 in 2024, though it wins 0.584 to
0.575 in 2023). With no current-season evidence, layer 1 collapses to the prior-season mean shrunk
toward a role — which is very nearly what the naive benchmark already is. The honest reading is that
**model v1 adds nothing before kickoff of week 1**, and that draft-day projections are a different
problem needing different inputs. Week 18 is worst for both, for the reason Phase 1 gave: playoff
teams rest starters, which is forecastable and which nothing here forecasts.

## Layer 1 on its own terms

Scored before it touched a single fantasy point, against "whatever his share was last week".

2024, held out:

| share | model rho | floor rho | model MAE | floor MAE | model RMSE | floor RMSE |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| snap | 0.843 | 0.831 | 0.1218 | 0.1238 | 0.1745 | 0.1889 |
| target | 0.789 | 0.740 | 0.0406 | 0.0474 | 0.0605 | 0.0730 |
| carry | 0.765 | **0.788** | 0.0358 | 0.0399 | 0.0783 | 0.0886 |
| pass attempt | 0.496 | **0.858** | 0.0151 | **0.0138** | 0.0852 | 0.0913 |

2023 reproduces every line of this within a hundredth, including both regressions.

**What got worse, and why.** Rank correlation on carry share and pass-attempt share is below the
floor, badly so for pass attempts. Two causes, both real:

1. **The tie block.** Actual carry share is exactly zero for most receivers; actual pass-attempt
   share is exactly zero or exactly one for almost everyone. The floor reproduces those exact values
   and inherits the tie structure. The model emits 0.02 and 0.85, breaking ties the actuals do not
   have, and average ranks punish it for inventing an ordering nobody claimed. On MAE and RMSE,
   where no tie structure is involved, the model wins on all four shares except pass-attempt MAE, by
   0.0013.
2. **A defect in the fit objective.** The search minimised error over three of the four shares the
   model emits. Pass-attempt share was never in the objective, and it is the one that regressed.

The fix for (2) is obvious and is **deliberately not applied**. The defect surfaced in the held-out
numbers, and re-fitting after reading them is precisely the tuning-against-the-test-set this phase
exists to avoid. It is the first thing Phase 3 should correct — on fit seasons, before 2024 is looked
at again — and the numbers above are what it should be compared against.

One further note on the fit itself: the first search minimised **mean absolute error** and ran to the
lowest shrinkage in the grid at every extension. That is the search saying it was asked the wrong
question — absolute error is minimised by the conditional median, and the share distribution is
dominated by bench players near zero, so MAE rewards predicting near zero for everybody and punishes
every ounce of regression. The objective was changed to RMSE, the proper loss for a conditional-mean
estimator, and the chosen point moved to the interior of the grid (decay 0.7, season shrinkage 2,
in-season 0.25). Both losses and the rank correlation are reported regardless: the loss chooses the
parameters, it does not get to choose what is published.

## Layer 2: what the touchdown measurement actually said

Regression constants, in opportunities of prior each rate is worth. Fitted on 2021–2022.

| rate | opportunities of prior |
|---|---|
| rushing yards per carry | 88 |
| receptions per target | 109 |
| receiving yards per target | 218 |
| passing yards per attempt | 252 |
| interceptions per attempt | 1,104 |
| **receiving touchdowns per target** | **1,123** |
| **rushing touchdowns per carry** | **10,000** (ceiling) |
| **passing touchdowns per attempt** | **10,000** (ceiling) |
| fumbles lost per play | 10,000 (ceiling) |

Every touchdown rate is regressed more than five times harder than any rate carrying bulk fantasy
points. The ceiling is not "regressed a lot": it means the variance decomposition found **no
persistent difference between players above sampling noise**, whose honest reading is "regress this
entirely to the role".

**The plan asked for a test pinning that touchdown rate regresses harder than any other rate. That
is false, and the test says so.** Fumble rate ties with two of the three at the ceiling, for exactly
the same reason — it is pure noise too. The pinned assertion is therefore the strongest true one:
every touchdown rate is regressed more than four times harder than every rate with real player
signal, and fumble rate is allowed to tie. Weakening the test to pass would have been wrong;
asserting something the measurement does not support would have been worse.

## What the measurement rests on

- **Point-in-time throughout.** Every week is replayed from the moment the previous week's numbers
  landed, through `observationsAsOf`. The structural test that matters: a week-2 replay is
  **byte-identical** before and after weeks 4 and 5 are written into the store with wildly different
  numbers (`src/projections/model/source.test.ts`). A model that trains on the week it predicts
  looks excellent and is worthless, and nothing else in the suite would notice — the scoreboard
  would simply improve.
- **Both sources see identical history.** `replayHeadToHead` replays each week once and shows every
  source the same rows, so the only difference between the two columns is the model.
- **Composed through real scoring rules.** The model predicts a `StatLine`; PPR composes it. The same
  model works unchanged in a superflex TE-premium league.
- **Reproducible.** A replay run twice over the same store is byte-identical, and every fitted
  parameter is committed.

### The floor moved slightly, and here is why

Phase 1 recorded the 2024 naive floor as rho 0.566 over 4,734 player-weeks. It now measures 0.569
over 4,767. Nothing about the benchmark changed: the historical player universe (Phase 2, Task 1)
resolves 99.5% of 2024 against the old name join's 98.1%, so a few dozen more player-weeks are in
the sample. The fringe band moved most (0.100 to 0.187), which is what one expects when the players
added are the marginal ones. **Every comparison in this document uses the re-measured floor**, taken
from the same run as the model it is compared to.

## What is NOT in this model, and is not pretended to be

- **Route participation and red-zone share.** Both are named in the spec's layer 1 and neither has a
  free source. Routes run is charting data nflverse does not publish; red-zone share needs
  play-by-play, which is a separate ingestion. `routes_run` is stored as null rather than zero
  precisely so nothing downstream mistakes absence for evidence. Red-zone share is the largest
  single gap, because it is the one thing that would make touchdown rate partly predictable rather
  than entirely regressed.
- **Everything above layer 3.** Opponent, game script, weather, rest, injuries, news. Phases 3 and 4.
- **Draft-day projections.** Week 1 is the one week the model does not beat the floor. A preseason
  projection is a different problem.
- **Kickers and team defences.** Phase 2 projects QB, RB, WR and TE. Those fields compose to exactly
  zero and are never handed a kicker.

## What to do next, in order

1. **Put pass-attempt share in the fit objective**, refit on 2021–2022, and re-measure. It is the one
   clearly identified defect and the fix is known.
2. **Cut error in the starter band (51–150).** It is the only band that regressed and it is where a
   manager's weekly start/sit decisions live. Confirm first whether the 2024 regression reproduces on
   a third season.
3. **Red-zone share**, from play-by-play. The only route to making touchdown rate partly predictable
   rather than wholly regressed.
4. **Band by a common prior (ADP)** so band-by-band comparison between sources is exact rather than
   indicative.
